# Truth vs Effectiveness — the Jurisdiction of "Correct"

**Summary**: A resolution of the puzzle *"why value truth if it isn't effective?"* — the word **correct** silently names three different things, and truth's authority is **domain-specific**: absolute over *means* (a false means is just false) and zero over *ends* (there is no true answer to what you should want). The confusion always turns out to be one of three: a false convention mistaken for a truth, a true law whose bill arrives on a delay, or an efficiency verdict aimed at a terminal want.

- Conversation with David, 2026-07-04 (three worked examples: prescriptive grammar → the Hebrew king's marriage law → a family trip). Authored, not source-ingested.
- Descriptive vs prescriptive grammar — standard descriptivist linguistics (the science describes usage; prescriptive rules are conventions, several imported from Latin and never true of English).
- Deuteronomy 17:14–20 (the law of the king); 1 Kings 4:25; 11:1–13; 12 (Solomon multiplies wives → heart turns → kingdom splits under Rehoboam).
- David Hume — the is/ought gap and instrumental reason ("reason is... the slave of the passions"): reason serves ends, it does not set them.
- Meadows on **delays** as a leverage point — see meadows-12-leverage-points.

The starting question sounds cynical but is real: *if a rule isn't effective — if no one follows it, or following it costs more than breaking it — why does it matter whether it's "correct"?* The answer is not "truth always wins." The answer is that **"correct" is three different words wearing one coat**, and only the middle one is a genuine truth-vs-effectiveness clash. Naming which one you're holding dissolves the puzzle every time.

This page owns the **Jurisdiction of Correct** framework: a three-pole map of *where truth-claims have authority over what we do*, plus two diagnostics for telling the poles apart.

## First, split the two axes people fuse

Two independent things get collapsed into "correct":

- **True** — corresponds to reality. A property of *claims about means*: does this rule track how the world works?
- **Effective** — achieves a goal. And "effective" itself hides two more senses that must be split:
  - **obeyed** — do people actually comply? (a fact about behaviour)
  - **works** — does compliance produce the promised result? (a fact about the world)

A rule can be *true but disobeyed* (everyone ignores it, and pays). A rule can be *obeyed but false* (everyone follows it, and it does nothing). "Not effective" is ambiguous across all three — and the ambiguity is where the puzzle lives.

## The three poles

| Pole | Worked example | What "correct" actually is | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 · False convention** | Prescriptive grammar ("don't split an infinitive") | A **norm mistaken for a truth** | **Drop it** | Break it → *nothing happens*, because it was never true |
| **2 · True law (delayed bill)** | The king's law: *don't multiply wives* (Deut 17:17) | A **true causal law with a lag** | **Keep it** | Break it → heart turns, kingdom splits — the cost is real but *late* |
| **3 · Terminal want** | The family trip everyone calls "not worth it" | An **efficiency verdict aimed at an end** | **No jurisdiction** | "Worth it?" measures a *means*; the trip is the *end* |

Only **Pole 2** is a real truth-vs-effectiveness collision. Pole 1 is a false alarm (the "truth" wasn't one); Pole 3 is a category error (truth was never the relevant authority).

### Pole 1 — the false convention

In linguistics the **truth** about a language is the *descriptive* grammar: how its speakers actually use it. **Prescriptive** rules ("don't end on a preposition," "don't split an infinitive") are mostly not truths at all — several were imported from Latin in the 1700s and were never true of English. So the folk-observation *"no one uses prescribed grammar"* doesn't show truth losing to effectiveness. It shows a **convention masquerading as a law**, correctly detected as hollow. The descriptive form is simultaneously the true one *and* the effective one — there was never a conflict, only a mislabel. The tell: **break the rule and nothing breaks.** No mechanism, no cost, no bill. That absence *is* the signature of a mere convention.

### Pole 2 — the true law whose bill is delayed

Deuteronomy 17:17 does not merely prohibit; it names a **mechanism**: the king must not multiply wives *"that his heart turn not away."* A causal claim with a stated failure mode. Then Kings runs the experiment. Solomon takes 700 wives and 300 concubines, and *"when Solomon was old, his wives turned away his heart after other gods"* (1 Kings 11:3–4) — the exact predicted failure, almost verbatim. The law was not *refuted* by his disobedience; it was **demonstrated** by it. Solomon is the proof that it was true.

Crucially, breaking it *was* effective — locally. Every foreign wife was a treaty (Pharaoh's daughter, Moab, Ammon, Edom, Sidon, the Hittites — 1 Kings 11:1); multiplying wives was Iron-Age foreign policy that bought the alliances and the golden age where *"Judah and Israel dwelt safely, every man under his vine and fig tree"* (1 Kings 4:25). Obeying meant **surrendering a locally-effective instrument** for a longer-range truth (undivided loyalty → undivided kingdom).

So why does it *look* like truth lost? **The delay** — and the text says so aloud: *"in thy days I will not do it... I will rend it out of the hand of thy son"* (1 Kings 11:12–13). Solomon banks the benefit and dies inside the golden age; the bill lands on Rehoboam, and the kingdom tears in two (1 Kings 12). The truth was perfectly effective — it just **invoiced the next generation.** People conclude "the rule didn't matter" because they read the receipt before the bill arrives. This is exactly Meadows' point about **delays**: systems where cause and effect are far apart in time are the ones humans manage worst (meadows-12-leverage-points).

### Pole 3 — the terminal want (no jurisdiction)

A family knows a trip is "expensive and not worth it," agrees to go anyway, and — in the questioner's words — "doesn't care what is correct." This one is *not* truth-vs-effectiveness at all; the axis changed from **effective** to **wants**, and that shift is the whole point.

*"Worth it"* measures the trip as a **means** — dollars in, value out. But for a family the trip usually *is* the end: the point is the being-together and the memory, not a return. Asking "is the trip worth it?" then has the same shape as "is loving my child cost-effective?" — malformed. Going anyway is not defying a truth; it is **rejecting a metric that was measuring the wrong thing** — repricing the trip in a currency (togetherness) that dollars can't denominate.

Hume's formulation: reason is *instrumental*. It can tell you *how* to reach what you value; it cannot tell you *what* to value. There is no true answer to what a family should want. So *"correct vs. wants"* is subtly a mis-frame — it sounds like a fight one side should win. It is not a fight; it is a **jurisdiction** question. Correct governs means. Wants govern ends. The trip lives among ends, so wants rule, and "correct" was never the authority there.

## Two diagnostics

Each pole has a test that separates it from its impostor.

**A · The mechanism-cost test** — *truth vs. convention* (tells Pole 1 from Pole 2). Break the rule and ask: is there a **mechanism-driven downstream cost**, even a delayed one?
- Split an infinitive → no mechanism, no cost → **convention** → safe to drop.
- Multiply wives → heart turns → covenant breaks → nation splits → **law** → never safe; the "effectiveness" of breaking it is a **loan, not income**.

**B · The reflective-endorsement test** — *terminal want vs. akrasia* (guards Pole 3 from becoming a licence). "We just wanted to" can be a genuine terminal want *or* a costume for **akrasia** — a known-bad means wearing the mask of an end ("I knew I shouldn't; I did it anyway"). Test: **do it, pay the bill in full, and ask whether you still endorse it.**
- The trip you'd take again ("the kids still talk about it") → **terminal want** → correct had no jurisdiction; the family was right.
- The splurge you disown ("I knew better") → **akrasia** → correct *did* have jurisdiction and lost. That is the real failure the cynic is pointing at — and it hides in the same words a terminal want uses.

## The answer to the original question

*Why is it important to know the truth if it isn't effective?* Because **truth's authority is domain-specific**:

- Over **means**, it is absolute — a false means is simply false, and its "effectiveness" is either an illusion (Pole 1: nothing was ever gained) or a loan against a delayed bill (Pole 2: the gain is real but borrowed).
- Over **ends**, it has none — there is no true answer to what to want, so a want can never be *refuted* by a correctness argument, only mis-served by one (Pole 3).

Every folk-intuition that "truth doesn't matter" is really one **domain confusion**: mistaking a convention for a truth, reading a delayed bill as no bill, or letting a spreadsheet price a memory. And the deeper reason to hold the true model even when a hack is locally effective: **truth is the effectiveness that generalizes and survives change.** A hack works for one goal in one situation; a true model works for goals you don't have yet, and when the hack breaks it is the only thing that tells you *why*. See knowledge-portfolio (true principles as non-expiring assets) and [surf-dive-learning-domains](./surf-dive-learning-domains.md) (the instrumental "what/how" surface vs. the "why/whose-frame" depth where ends are examined).

## METER

- `jurisdiction_misfile` — count decisions where an *efficiency verdict* was aimed at a *terminal good*, or a *convention* was defended as a *law*. Review weekly; target trending toward zero as the three-pole reflex sets.
- `delayed_bill_named` — for any "effective now" choice with a suspected Pole-2 shape, log the predicted delayed cost **and a review date** at decision time. Pass = the bill is *named before* it arrives, not discovered after.
- `endorsement_verdict` — for "we just wanted to" choices, run the reflective-endorsement test once the bill is paid; tag each **terminal** (endorsed) or **akrasia** (disowned). A rising akrasia share is the signal that "wanting" has become a licence.

## Mnemonic

A **Velvet Aeon** ([world-velvet-aeon](./world-velvet-aeon.md)) courtroom, watercolor, dusk light through tall windows. On the bench sits a single robed judge whose nameplate reads **CORRECT**. Three defendants are brought up in turn, three doors behind them:

1. A **schoolteacher** clutches a cracked Latin grammar; the judge taps her rulebook with a small **hammer** and it gives a *dead thud* — "not my jurisdiction; this was never true" — and she dissolves like wet ink (the false convention).
2. **King Solomon** in gold, seven hundred wedding rings threaded on a chain, his painted heart visibly rotating to face *away*; the hammer strikes his law and it **rings like a struck bell that keeps ringing** — the gavel falls, but an **hourglass** hands the written sentence past Solomon to a **young man behind him** (Rehoboam) as a map of the kingdom tears down the middle (the true law, billed late).
3. A **family with a suitcase** stands at an open door that leads *out of the courthouse entirely*, onto a bright sea; the judge sets the gavel **down** — "no jurisdiction over wanting" — because the door is outside his walls (the terminal want).

Two instruments stay on the bench: the **hammer** that makes a rule ring or thud (mechanism-cost test), and a **mirror** angled at each defendant showing their future self *after the bill is paid* — the family smiles back (endorse); a gambler-double in the corner looks away (disown). One judge, two tools, three doors.
