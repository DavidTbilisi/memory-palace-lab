# Position Essay

**Summary**: The short piece where you are handed a **proposition** — not a question — and must commit to a position and defend it with arguments *and* examples, under a clock. The wiki owned written reception ([semantic-reading-system](./semantic-reading-system.md)) and written workplace persuasion (persuasive-writing-and-influence), and nothing for this: an essay with no reader to act, no ask to make, and a grader instead of an audience. Five moves — **Stand · Because · Show · Yet · So** — and one load-bearing rule the neighbouring pages do not state: **every argument owes an example that the argument cannot supply by itself.**

## What it is, and what it is not

A position essay answers a **stated proposition** with a committed stance and a defence. It is short — the NAEC floor is a hundred words — and it is written against a clock, for a reader who will never act on it.

That last clause is the whole distinguisher. persuasive-writing-and-influence is the wiki's owner for written persuasion and it is built around an **ask**: its execution order ends in a recommendation with an owner and a timing, and its success metric is reader-action-taken rate. It also scopes itself explicitly away from expressive writing. A position essay has no ask, no owner, no timing, and nobody to take an action — the reader is a grader. Its machinery therefore cannot score this piece, which is precisely why the piece needed a page.

What transfers from that page and is **not** restated here: claim in sentence one, the repair pass (read aloud · hostile reader · cut 20%), and its failure modes for warmup paragraphs, buried ledes and hedging-as-humility. Use them from there.

## The prompt is a proposition, and it comes in two shapes

Read the prompt's grammar before choosing a side, because one of the two shapes hands you an extra position for free.

**Bare proposition.** *"In a tense, critical situation a photojournalist should put the camera aside and help the person in danger"* (source: ანალიტიკური წერა - I ვარიანტი.pdf). A claim with no reason attached. Your options are agree, disagree, or qualify.

**Proposition plus its own reason.** *"Keeping a pet in an apartment, in a closed space, is not advisable, **because** it poses a danger to human health"* (source: ანალიტიკური წერა - II ვარიანტი.pdf). The prompt has supplied its own support — and support can be attacked separately from the claim. This opens a third position unavailable in the bare shape: **accept the conclusion, reject the stated reason.** One may well hold that pets are unsuited to small flats without granting that health risk is why. That essay is more interesting than either agreement or refusal, it demonstrates exactly the analytic control Task I tests, and it is invisible unless you notice the *because*.

A prompt whose reason you reject also lets you refuse the claim by attacking only the support — a cheaper target than the claim itself, and the one [copi-analyzing-arguments](./copi-analyzing-arguments.md) §Combined vs parallel support says to aim at when the support is combined.

## The commit move

The single most expensive failure in this form is never landing. An essay that surveys both sides evenly and closes with *"therefore the question is complex"* has answered nothing, and it is the natural output of a thoughtful person under time pressure who can see both sides — which is to say, the failure selects for exactly the people who could write the best version.

Commit in sentence one.

**A qualified position is still a position.** *"Yes, except where the photographer is the only record"* commits; *"there are arguments on both sides"* does not. The test is whether a reader could act on it if action were possible: a qualification names the boundary of your claim, a hedge conceals that you have not made one. Qualify on what is genuinely uncertain and state the rest flatly.

## The five moves

| Move | What it does | Rough share of a 100–200 word piece |
|---|---|---|
| **Stand** | The position, sentence one, no warmup | 1 sentence |
| **Because** | The reason that carries it — the argument proper | 1–2 sentences |
| **Show** | The example that does work the reason cannot | 1–2 sentences |
| **Yet** | The strongest opposing case, granted and then bounded | 1–2 sentences |
| **So** | The close: a consequence, not a summary | 1 sentence |

They read as a single chain — *I stand here, because…, as shown by…, yet…, so…* — which is why the mnemonic is the sentence itself. At the hundred-word floor each move is roughly one sentence; with more room, **Because** and **Show** take the extra, never **Stand**.

## The rule that nothing else owns: arguments owe examples

The NAEC instruction names both, in both variants: *bring arguments **and** examples to substantiate your position* (sources: ანალიტიკური წერა - I ვარიანტი.pdf, ანალიტიკური წერა - II ვარიანტი.pdf). Most writing treats the second as garnish on the first. It is not, and the test is mechanical:

> **Delete the example. If the argument is exactly as strong without it, the example was decoration and costs you words you do not have.**

A working example does something the general claim cannot: it supplies the *case where the abstraction is tested*. Three forms are reliably available under a clock, in rising order of cost:

- **The concrete case.** A situation specific enough to be checkable — a named instance if you have one, an unambiguous generic one if you do not. It converts *"a photographer's presence can change the outcome"* into a scene in which it does.
- **The counterfactual.** Remove or reverse the thing at issue and say what changes. This is the same move as [Mill's Method of Difference](./causal-reasoning-mill-methods.md), borrowed into prose, and it is the strongest example available when you have no case to cite — which, in an exam room with no sources, is usual.
- **The contrasting pair.** Two situations alike in everything but the one factor, landing differently. Expensive in words; buys the most when the proposition turns on a distinction rather than on a quantity.

**A worked instance is not the same as a reused instance.** The example must be attached to *your* reason. An example that illustrates the proposition in general, but not the specific reason you gave for your position, reads as padding to anyone checking whether the parts connect.

## Granting the objection, and the close

**Yet — take the strongest objection, not the most convenient one.** Choosing a weak opponent is the straw-man failure in [fallacy-taxonomy](./fallacy-taxonomy.md), and it is visible: a grader who can name a better objection than the one you handled has just found the ceiling of your essay. Grant the objection its real force in its own words, then **bound** it — say where it holds and why that region does not cover your case. Bounding is the move; refuting is usually unavailable in two sentences and attempting it is what makes the move collapse back into assertion.

**So — a close is a consequence, not a summary.** At a hundred words the reader has not forgotten your position and does not need it restated. Spend the last sentence on what follows if you are right: what it commits us to, where the line now falls, what changes. A summary close is the most common way to waste the highest-value sentence in the piece.

## The budget, and the trap peculiar to this task

Thirty-five minutes for a hundred words is an extraordinary allowance — roughly twenty times what the writing itself costs. Every other section of common-masters-exam is time-starved; this one is time-rich, and the failure mode inverts with it.

| Phase | Minutes | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Choose | 5 | Read the prompt's grammar, take the position, find the example *before* writing |
| Write | 15 | The five moves, in order, no re-reading |
| Repair | 10 | persuasive-writing-and-influence's pass: read aloud, hostile reader, cut 20% |
| Buffer | 5 | Left deliberately unspent |

**The floor is not a target.** A hundred words is the threshold below which the piece is not graded against all criteria — it is the point at which scoring *starts*, not the length being asked for. But the opposite error is the likelier one here: with thirty-five minutes and nothing to compute, the natural drift is to keep writing, add a third argument, qualify the qualification, and end with a longer, weaker, unbounded piece. **More words past the point where the five moves are complete make the essay worse, not longer.** The repair pass exists to enforce that, and the ten minutes allotted to it are not padding.

## What this page does not own

**The rubric.** NAEC's scoring criteria, weights and thresholds for this task are unverified and sit on the standing ⚠ block in common-masters-exam. The instruction's phrase — graded *"according to all criteria"* — proves criteria exist and says nothing about what they are. Everything above is derived from the task's stated demands (a proposition, a position, arguments, examples, a word floor) and from general argument structure. If the real rubric ever surfaces, this page is the one to check against it, and the five moves are the claim most exposed to being wrong.

## Failure modes

1. **The balanced survey.** Both sides weighed, no position taken, closing on complexity. The form's defining failure, and it selects for careful thinkers.
2. **Example as decoration.** An instance that restates the argument instead of testing it. Apply the deletion test.
3. **The convenient objection.** Conceding to a weak opponent while a stronger one stands unhandled.
4. **Summary close.** Spending the last sentence repeating the first.
5. **Missing the *because*.** Treating a proposition-with-reason as a bare proposition, and never noticing the third position it offered.
6. **Writing to the clock instead of to the moves.** Thirty-five minutes of available time consumed because it was available.
7. **Hedge disguised as nuance.** Qualification that names no boundary. See §The commit move for the test.

## METER

| Event | Measures | Pass floor |
|---|---|---|
| `essay.commit_latency` | minutes from reading the prompt to a written position sentence | **≤ 5 min**, and a position that passes the boundary test |
| `essay.example_yield` | arguments carrying an example that survives the deletion test, over arguments made | **1.0** — every argument, no exceptions |
| `essay.timed_write` | a complete five-move piece, ≥100 words, under 35 minutes, repair pass included | all five moves present; **Yet** handles an objection the writer can defend as the strongest available |

`essay.example_yield` is the one to watch, because it is the only floor here that a fluent writer fails. Commit latency and completion both improve with ordinary practice; the deletion test is adversarial against your own prose and stays uncomfortable, which under [desirable-difficulties](./desirable-difficulties.md) is the signal that it is the one doing work.

These floors are provisional and self-scored. Unlike the exam's three multiple-choice sections, this task has no key and no scorer — which cuts both ways: it is the only section a timed sitting *can* grade honestly, since the criteria above are structural and checkable by the writer, and it is the section where the actual rubric remains unknown.

## Mnemonic

**🪧 Stand, because, show, yet, so.**

A placard has room for one line, so the line has to be a position. Picture yourself holding one up in a square: you say where you stand, why, you point at the thing everyone can see that proves it, you grant the loudest heckler their one good point and say where it stops — and then you say what follows, and put the placard down. You do not read the placard aloud again before leaving.

*The chain is the outline. Say it and you have written it.*

The six faces — [CAST](./cast-overview.md) · [NEDF](./nedf-overview.md) · [SPEAR](./spear-overview.md) · [HEART](./heart-overview.md) · [ORACLE](./oracle-overview.md) · [GRACE](./grace-overview.md) — for this page:

## U — See (CAST)

1. Proposition → position → defence → close: a chain, not a balance
2. Each argument node carries exactly one example edge; an argument with no such edge is unsupported

## D — Name (NEDF)

1. Position essay = a committed stance on a stated proposition, defended with paired arguments and examples, short and timed
2. Distinguisher: vs persuasive-writing-and-influence, there is no ask and no reader action — the reader is a grader, so reader-action metrics cannot score it
3. Failure mode: the balanced survey that never lands, which is what a careful thinker produces under pressure

## F — Do (SPEAR)

1. Read the prompt's grammar → is there a *because*? → choose among three positions, not two
2. Commit in sentence one → then the four remaining moves in order
3. Every argument written → apply the deletion test to its example before moving on

## B — Watch (HEART)

1. A closing sentence that restates the opening one
2. The **Yet** move aimed at an objection weaker than the one available
3. Words still being added after all five moves are complete

## L — Predict (ORACLE)

1. Deletion test applied honestly → predict a shorter, stronger piece; skipped → predict length without strength
2. Position committed in sentence one → predict the remaining four moves write themselves; deferred → predict the balanced survey

## R — Act (GRACE)

1. Prompt read → position sentence written before any other sentence, always
2. Five moves complete → stop writing and start the repair pass, whatever time remains
