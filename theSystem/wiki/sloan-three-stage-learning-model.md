# Sloan Three-Stage Informal Learning Model

**Summary**: Sloan's three-stage cycle for the **informal learning that produces strategic thinking**: **Preparation · Experience · Reevaluation**. Each stage has both an **affective** (emotional) component and a **cognitive** (analytical) component — the affective half is load-bearing and is what most strategy training omits. The stages are iterative and "messy" — they don't run in sequence; the strategist oscillates among them. Reflection runs through all three but plays a distinct role in each. The 5 [attributes](./five-attributes-strategic-thinking.md) operate in interplay throughout.

**Sources**:
- [sloan-learning-to-think-strategically](./sloan-learning-to-think-strategically.md) — source summary
- Sloan, *Learning to Think Strategically* (4th ed., 2020), Ch 7 §"Three-stage model for learning strategic thinking", Figures 7.1 and 7.2

## The three stages

| Stage | Affective component | Cognitive component | Primary outputs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Preparation** | Must-win attitude; emotional readiness; conviction; fear of losing what you love | Information gathering; data; analysis; retrieval of past knowledge | Decision to engage; named purpose; baseline data |
| **Experience** | Tension; engagement; excitement; the "dress rehearsal" feeling | Drawing on **current experience** + **prior successful life experience** (often non-work) as a metaphor for the new situation; framing; testing | New strategy hypothesis tested against patterns; analogies surfaced |
| **Reevaluation** | Fatigue · pride · relief · concern; the *act-think-talk-think-act loop* | Validation of prior learning; "checking" the strategic decisions; new perspectives surfaced; reframing | Confirmed or revised strategy; new questions; identified learning |

## Why "informal"

Strategic thinking is learned **informally**, not formally. Sloan's executive interviews are emphatic — none of the executives credited formal courses with their learning to think strategically; many actively dismissed formal training as irrelevant.

> Most of what I learned in courses was only technique — like a nice craft. (Japanese financial executive)

What they credited: travel, prior non-work successes (farming, painting, sculpting, hot-air ballooning, racing, photography), critical dialogue with strangers, reflection on lived experience.

The informal pattern is the *substrate*; the 3 stages give it operational structure. They turn "experience just happens" into "experience can be cultivated".

## The affective component — load-bearing

This is the single most underappreciated insight in the model. Most strategy education treats emotion as **noise**; Sloan's evidence says it's the **signal**.

> Traditionally, the role of the affective in strategy development has been viewed either as an inhibitor of learning, due to anxiety and stress, or as something that can be brought into conscious awareness as an "object" for rational analysis. The former has generated an emphasis on creating safe learning environments for discussion; the latter has led to psychological and therapeutic approaches aimed at dissecting the issue so that strategic thinking can proceed.

Sloan's correction: the affective is neither inhibitor nor object — it is the **driver and the substrate**. Specifically:

- **Preparation**: The must-win attitude (affective) is what initiates the cycle. Without it, the strategist does not even begin. Fear, conviction, passion, and "fear of losing what you love" are the named affective forms.
- **Experience**: Tension during experience is what produces learning. Comfortable experience produces no learning.
- **Reevaluation**: Pride, relief, and concern during reevaluation are what consolidate or revise the learning. Emotionless reevaluation produces forgetting.

> The heart has reasons …​ written in a language different from the formal, explicit, logical one in which our minds operate. Discerning how one's instincts define a situation is a matter of translation. It is an interpretive art. (Boud and Walker, quoted by Sloan)

## Why the cycle is "messy"

The stages **don't run in sequence**. A strategist may:
- Start in Experience (something happens) → realize they didn't Prepare → loop back
- Spend weeks in Preparation (intentionally) before any Experience
- Reevaluate continuously during Experience (reflection-in-action; see [surf-dive-learning-domains](./surf-dive-learning-domains.md))
- Have one stage stretch over months while another takes seconds

Sloan's word: *messy*. The 3-stage diagram is a topology, not a schedule.

## Prior successful life experience — the substrate

A consistent finding from Sloan's interviews: every successful strategist could name a **prior successful life experience** in a **non-work** domain that they used as the metaphor for the new strategic problem. Examples:

- A farmer who "knew strategy making" before he knew the word — applied farming experience to corporate strategy
- A Japanese executive who used auto-racing as his strategy metaphor since junior high
- A Japanese banker who used painting as his strategy metaphor
- An American CEO who used sculpture
- A Swiss financial investor who used orchestra rehearsal
- A Danish executive who used ceramics

The substrate matters because it carries the **embodied, tacit knowledge** that no formal training delivers. The 3-stage cycle is most effective when the strategist has a clear **prior successful life experience** to anchor against.

**The implication**: developing strategic thinkers should include identifying and explicitly using their prior successful life experiences as metaphors — not pretending those experiences are irrelevant to "business".

## The 8-phase Marsick/Watkins experiential learning sub-cycle

Sloan cites Marsick & Watkins's 8-phase model that runs inside the Experience stage:

1. Framing the experience based on past experiences
2. Diagnosing the new experience
3. Interpreting the context
4. Deciding on a solution
5. Drawing on or developing skills
6. Producing a solution
7. Assessing consequences
8. Drawing further conclusions

The 8-phase sub-cycle is finer-grained than the 3 stages but maps inside Experience. Useful for after-the-fact reconstruction; not necessary for everyday operation.

## METER

- `preparation_affective_named` — did the must-win attitude get named explicitly at the start? Boolean; pass ≥80% of strategy cycles
- `prior_experience_metaphor_used` — was a non-work prior successful experience invoked? Boolean; pass ≥1 per major cycle
- `reevaluation_completed_flag` — did the cycle close with explicit reevaluation (or did it just end)? Pass ≥90%
- `iterative_loop_count` — how many times did the cycle re-enter Preparation from Reevaluation? Pass ≥2 for major problems

## Failure modes

1. **Cognitive-only Preparation** — gathering data without the affective component; produces strategic-planning-grade preparation, not strategic-thinking-grade. Defense: explicit affective name-check at start.
2. **Experience-skipping** — jumping from Preparation to Reevaluation without lived Experience; produces armchair strategy. Defense: structured Experience phase mandatory.
3. **Reevaluation-skipping** — most common failure; the strategy executes and the team moves on without explicit reevaluation. Defense: [AAR](./after-action-review.md) as mandatory close.
4. **Single-pass thinking** — running the 3 stages once and declaring done; misses the iterative nature. Defense: minimum 2 cycles for any non-trivial strategy.
5. **Prior-experience-dismissal** — treating non-work successes as irrelevant to "real" strategy; loses the metaphor substrate. Defense: identify each strategist's prior successful life experience explicitly.

## Mnemonic

A Velvet Aeon Cosmic-mode **triple-arched gateway**, each arch a different shape:

- **Preparation arch** — tall and narrow, framed by **two braziers** (one orange-flame = affective; one blue-flame = cognitive). A woman stands before the arch, **one hand on each brazier**, simultaneous. Her face is **strong** (per [feedback-image-face-and-hair](./feedback-image-face-and-hair.md); she must want to enter).
- **Experience arch** — wide and asymmetric, **shifting shape** like water. The same woman walks through it, **dragging a long ribbon of memory** behind her (the prior successful life experience). Inside the arch, **shadows of other figures** (analog selves) move alongside her — the metaphor substrate in action.
- **Reevaluation arch** — circular, **mirror-walled**, surrounded by **9 small floating lanterns** (the [9 dive questions](./nine-dive-question-drill.md)). The woman emerges from this arch carrying a **revised scroll** (the revised strategy). The mirrors reflect her *previous* selves from earlier loops.

A **silver thread** loops from the Reevaluation arch back to the Preparation arch — the iterative loop. The woman re-enters Preparation from Reevaluation, **carrying the lessons** as a small flame in her cupped hand. Long hair lifted; one overhead moon; no atmospheric noise.

## Memory checksum

- **3** stages (Preparation · Experience · Reevaluation)
- **2** components per stage (affective + cognitive); **6** total components
- **8** Marsick/Watkins phases inside Experience (sub-cycle, optional)
- **1** load-bearing insight (affective is signal, not noise)
- **1** substrate type (prior successful life experience, usually non-work)
- **5** failure modes
- **1** iterative-loop requirement (≥2 cycles for non-trivial strategy)

If you can recite **3-2(×3=6)-8-1-1-5-1** in 25 seconds and *name your own prior successful life experience that anchors your strategy thinking*, the page is encoded.
