# Scene Grammar

**Summary**: Two-layer framework for analyzing and constructing visual scenes — the seven **Elements of Art** as the *vocabulary* (Line · Shape · Form · Value · Color · Space · Texture) and the nine **Principles of Design** as the *grammar* (Pattern · Contrast · Emphasis · Balance · Proportion/Scale · Harmony · Rhythm · Unity · Variety). Elements = what to perceive and manipulate; Principles = how to compose multiple elements into a coherent whole. Direct theory layer for [visualization-training](./visualization-training.md) Stage 3 (Elements map 1:1 to manipulation domains) and Stage 4 (Principles are the composition rules for multi-element hub scenes). The page was previously titled "Visual Art Fundamentals" — renamed to make the load-bearing claim ("this is the grammar of every scene") legible from the title alone.

## The Two-Layer Architecture

Every visual scene — real or mental — can be analyzed and constructed at two levels:

| Layer | What it is | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| **Elements** | Raw perceptual materials; the building blocks you can isolate and name | Vocabulary |
| **Principles** | Rules for arranging elements to achieve visual goals | Grammar |

The split is the same one [color-theory-mental-model](./color-theory-mental-model.md) makes within the color domain alone: Hue/Value/Saturation are *color elements*; Complementary/Analogous/Triadic are *color principles*. This page extends the same architecture to the full visual scene.

**Practical implication**: when a mental image feels wrong or weak, diagnose at the right level. If an *element* is wrong (wrong value, wrong texture), fix the element. If the *composition* feels wrong (clashing, chaotic, flat), apply a principle fix (adjust contrast, add an emphasis point, unify temperature).

## The Seven Elements of Art

Each element is a distinct perceptual axis. You can isolate any one of them, manipulate it independently, and observe the result.

### 1 — Line

A mark on a surface that describes a shape or outline. Lines have **direction** (vertical, horizontal, diagonal, curved), **quality** (thick, thin, sharp, fuzzy), and **type** (actual lines you can see vs implied lines formed by alignment or eye movement).

- Vertical lines: stability, structure, hierarchy
- Horizontal lines: calm, rest, infinite space
- Diagonal lines: motion, tension, dynamism
- Curved lines: flow, softness, organic quality
- Implied lines: invisible trajectories that direct attention (a pointing finger, aligned objects)

**Visualization application** → Stage 3 State/Structure domain. A line sweep: trace the object's dominant contour, then shift from actual outlines to implied silhouette to pure gestural suggestion.

---

### 2 — Shape

A 2-dimensional enclosed area defined by a line or a change in value/color. Shapes have no thickness or depth.

| Type | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Geometric** | Regular, precise, mathematical | Circles, squares, triangles, polygons |
| **Organic** | Irregular, freeform, nature-derived | Clouds, leaves, blobs, human silhouettes |

**Visualization application** → Stage 3 State/Structure domain. Shape is the flat 2D silhouette of an object — the starting point before Form (see below) inflates it with volume.

---

### 3 — Form

A 3-dimensional object with **volume** and **thickness**. Form is what Shape becomes when light is added — the illusion of 3D depth on a 2D surface, or the actual 3D experience in mental imagery.

**Form = Shape + light/shadow + depth**

Key move: **Shape → Form inflation**. Take any flat 2D shape, add a light source, add shadow on the opposite side, and the shape becomes a 3D form. In mental imagery, this is the move from "I see a circle" to "I see a sphere."

**Visualization application** → Stage 3 Scale + State/Structure domains. Inflate a flat shape into a volumetric form by adding: (1) a light source direction, (2) shadow on the opposite face, (3) ambient occlusion at recessed edges, (4) specular highlight at the lit apex.

---

### 4 — Value

The degree of lightness and darkness — the full range from pure black through all gray tones to pure white. Value works with both color images and grayscale.

Value is the strongest single factor in **contrast** and **depth perception**. High-value-contrast scenes read as dramatic; low-value-contrast scenes read as soft or hazy. The same hue at different values produces different emotional weights.

**Cross-link to color theory**: Value is one of the three axes in the [color-theory-mental-model](./color-theory-mental-model.md) cylinder (the "slide" axis, black → white). The same manipulation drills apply here.

**Visualization application** → Stage 3 Color/Light domain (value sweep: pure black → gray → white → back to original). Also drives perceived depth: dark values recede, light values advance.

---

### 5 — Color

Color is made of three properties: **Hue** (the color family name), **Value** (lightness/darkness), and **Intensity** (brightness and purity). This element has its own complete mental model at [color-theory-mental-model](./color-theory-mental-model.md), which covers the 3D cylinder, the Čižiková 4-axis system, harmony patterns, and all Stage 3 color drills.

**Cross-link**: [color-theory-mental-model](./color-theory-mental-model.md) is the full owner of this element's drill domain.

---

### 6 — Space

The area around, within, and between objects. Space has two types:

| Type | What it is | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive space** | The objects, subjects, forms themselves | Subject; foreground; presence |
| **Negative space** | The voids, backgrounds, gaps between objects | Breathing room; context; framing |

Space also creates the illusion of depth through: overlap (near objects cover far ones), scale diminishment (far objects appear smaller), atmospheric perspective (far objects appear lower contrast and cooler), and placement (objects higher in the frame appear farther).

**Visualization application** → Stage 3 Space/Position domain. A space drill: scan the positive objects in a scene → then shift attention entirely to the negative space (the *between* and *around*) → then toggle between them.

---

### 7 — Texture

The surface quality of an object — how it would feel to the touch, or how it appears visually. Texture exists in two modes:

| Mode | Description | Sense used |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical texture** | Actual 3D surface variation (rough stone, smooth glass, fabric weave) | Touch |
| **Implied texture** | Visual pattern that *suggests* surface quality without literal depth (a photograph of wood grain) | Sight |

Texture axes to drill:
- **Tactile roughness**: ultra-smooth (polished marble) → satin → rough (stone) → coarse → jagged
- **Reflectivity**: matte (absorbs all light) → satin → glossy → mirror (reflects all light)
- **Material grain**: wood grain · fabric weave · stone pores · metal brushed/cast · skin pores · crystal facets
- **Scale**: zoom into texture until grain becomes landscape; zoom out until surface pattern becomes texture
- **Implied → physical**: a visual pattern on a flat surface becomes raised 3D relief

**Note on Stage 3 placement**: Texture moves (rough/smooth, matte/glossy) currently live inside the Material/Phase domain as sub-moves. They are called out here as their own element because they are perceptually independent: the *material* (wood vs metal) and the *texture* (rough vs polished) are separate axes — you can have polished wood or rough metal.

**Visualization application** → Stage 3 Material/Phase domain, Texture sub-axis. A texture sweep: take the seed object (apple) → run the full tactile roughness spectrum → run the full reflectivity spectrum → swap material grain families → zoom into micro-texture until you can see individual cells. Add REMAPS S: feel each texture change on the palm of your hand as you make it.

## The Nine Principles of Design

Where Elements answer "what is there?", Principles answer "how is it arranged?"

### 1 — Pattern

A regular arrangement of alternated or repeated elements, shapes, or motifs. Pattern emerges when a unit is tiled, alternated, or progressively varied.

**Visualization drill**: Take any element and repeat it with deliberate variation — same shape, different sizes in a grid; same color, different positions in a spiral; alternating rough/smooth strips. Zoom out: when does texture become pattern? Zoom in: when does pattern become texture?

---

### 2 — Contrast

The juxtaposition of strongly different elements — dark vs light, rough vs smooth, warm vs cool, large vs small, saturated vs muted. Contrast is the engine of visual attention: the eye goes where contrast is highest.

Contrast types: **Value contrast** (dark against light), **Color contrast** (warm against cool, complementary hues), **Texture contrast** (rough against smooth), **Scale contrast** (large against small), **Shape contrast** (geometric against organic).

**Visualization drill**: Construct a scene with one element at maximum contrast against its environment. Desaturate everything except one object. Place one rough object in an all-smooth field. Place one hot-color object in an all-cool palette. The contrasted element becomes the focal point automatically.

---

### 3 — Emphasis / Focal Point

Special importance given to one part of a scene, achieved through contrast, color, size, placement, or repetition. Emphasis creates the **focal point** — where the eye goes first and returns to.

Emphasis methods: high contrast value · saturated hue against muted field · largest object in frame · isolated object (surrounded by negative space) · brightest object in low-light scene · the only moving element · the only organic shape among geometric ones.

**Visualization drill**: Start with a neutral scene (5 similar objects). Select one and apply every emphasis method simultaneously: make it brighter, more saturated, larger, surrounded by space, and slightly animated. Watch how the eye is pulled there despite the other objects. Then gradually remove emphasis methods one at a time until the focal point dissolves.

---

### 4 — Balance

The arrangement of elements to create an impression of equality in weight or importance. Balance is felt, not calculated.

| Type | Description | Feel |
|---|---|---|
| **Symmetrical** | Mirror image left/right or top/bottom | Formal, stable, static |
| **Asymmetrical** | Different elements arranged to feel equally weighted | Dynamic, natural, interesting |
| **Radial** | Elements arranged around a central point | Unified, explosive, mandala-like |

**Visualization drill**: Symmetry — place your seed object, then mirror it exactly across a vertical axis. Asymmetry — place a large dark object on the left, then find how many small light objects on the right create equal visual weight. Radial — place a central element and repeat smaller elements outward in all directions.

---

### 5 — Proportion / Scale

The relationship between objects with respect to size and number — how parts relate to the whole, and how objects relate to each other.

Scale is absolute (how large or small an object is); Proportion is relative (how the sizes of elements compare to each other or to an expected standard).

**Visualization drill**: This maps directly to Stage 3 Scale domain. Add the relational layer: not just "make the apple huge" but "make the apple 10× the table it sits on." The disproportionate scale relationship is more visually striking than absolute size alone. Deliberate proportion violations (tiny giant, enormous insect) are the most memorable compositions.

---

### 6 — Harmony

The arrangement of elements so that all parts feel as though they belong together — a coherent whole rather than a collection of unrelated pieces. Harmony is the feeling of visual unity across the scene.

Sources of harmony: shared color temperature (all warm, or all cool), limited hue family (analogous palette), consistent texture register (all rough or all smooth), consistent value range (all mid-tones), shared line direction (all diagonal).

**Visualization drill**: Take a chaotic scene (multiple unrelated objects). Apply one unifying constraint — shift all objects to the same color temperature. Watch the scene cohere. Then shift to a single-hue monochromatic palette (vary only value and saturation). This is the mental equivalent of "everything bathed in golden sunset light."

---

### 7 — Rhythm / Movement

The use of recurring elements to direct the eye through a composition, creating the sense of visual flow. There are five kinds:

- **Random**: unplanned, irregular repetition (natural scatter)
- **Regular**: evenly spaced, predictable (grid, columns)
- **Alternating**: two elements swapping in sequence (A-B-A-B)
- **Progressive**: element changes with each repetition (growing, shrinking, shifting color)
- **Flowing**: curved, wave-like movement through the scene

**Visualization drill**: Progressive rhythm — repeat your seed object 5 times with each instance progressively smaller, darker, and more distant. The sequence creates a spatial path the eye follows. Flowing rhythm — a series of objects that curves and bends like a river, leading from one side of the scene to the other.

---

### 8 — Unity

All parts of an image connected so as to be experienced as one whole. Unity is the highest-level coherence — the gestalt. A unified scene has all its Elements and Principles in service of a single impression.

Unity is achieved when: repetition ties elements together, harmony makes colors belong to each other, emphasis establishes a clear hierarchy, and rhythm moves the eye without confusion.

**Visualization drill**: Build a scene from 3 unrelated objects. Apply one Element-level constraint (all the same value range) AND one Principle-level constraint (all oriented toward a single focal point). The scene should now feel like it belongs together even though the objects are different.

---

### 9 — Variety

The use of different elements within a composition to create visual interest. Variety is the counterweight to unity — without it, harmony becomes monotony; without unity, variety becomes chaos.

The optimal composition has unity as its foundation with variety as controlled disruption: one element that breaks the pattern, one hue that contrasts the dominant palette, one organic shape among geometric ones.

**Visualization drill**: Start with a perfectly harmonized monochromatic scene (all blues, all smooth, all similar size). Introduce one element that violates every constraint: warm orange, rough, much larger. The contrast reads as relief and delight, not chaos — because the unified foundation is strong enough to absorb it.

## Memorization — The Canonical Scene

The 7 Elements + 9 Principles are *meta*: they're the toolkit for constructing every visual scene. The strongest mnemonic is therefore a **single scene that contains all of them visibly**, so the scene IS the index AND the demonstration. The default style is [world-velvet-aeon](./world-velvet-aeon.md) unless overridden.

### Why scene-over-glyph here

Code memorization wants frozen polyomino glyphs (feedback_glyph_over_scene applies to *code*). Scene Grammar is a set of scene-construction rules — the canonical demonstration *must be a scene* because that's what the content teaches. The mnemonic and the lesson collapse into the same artifact. (This is the same reason [color-theory-mental-model](./color-theory-mental-model.md) uses the 3D cylinder as its mnemonic: the structure of the mnemonic mirrors the structure of the content.)

### The scene anchor

A mermaid-figure standing at a half-flooded stone arch, lit by one warm candle, against a vast empty nebula sky. (The figure follows the [feedback-image-figure-rule](./feedback-image-figure-rule.md) fragile/strong dichotomy; architecture matters more than the subject identity.) The scene is engineered so each Element is anchored to a specific visible feature and each Principle is the explicit design choice you can point at.

### 7 Elements → 7 visible anchors

| # | Element | Anchor in the canonical scene |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Line** | Flow of her hair + the horizon line of the waterline |
| 2 | **Shape** | Geometric stone arch vs. her organic silhouette (the geometric/organic contrast is itself the demonstration) |
| 3 | **Form** | Sphere/orb she holds — Shape inflated to 3D via key light + cast shadow |
| 4 | **Value** | High-contrast key light on her face → deep velvet shadow behind |
| 5 | **Color** | Tight monochrome palette ([color-theory-mental-model](./color-theory-mental-model.md)) |
| 6 | **Space** | Figure in positive space, vast empty sky as negative space |
| 7 | **Texture** | Wet skin vs. rough weathered stone vs. dissolved-into-water hair |

**Quick-recall hand drill** (no scene available, eyes closed): thumb=Line, index=Shape, middle=Form, ring=Value, pinky=Color, palm=Space, back-of-hand=Texture. Walk it twice/day for one week and the ordered list becomes reflexive.

### 9 Principles → the scene's design choices

The Principles are not anchored to individual features — they are the *rules the scene already follows*. Re-walking the scene and asking "why is it composed this way?" derives all nine:

| # | Principle | What the scene does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Pattern** | Repeating ripples on the water surface |
| 2 | **Contrast** | Bright lit face against velvet-black shadow (max value contrast) |
| 3 | **Emphasis** | The single warm candle pulls the eye first; the orb is the secondary focal point |
| 4 | **Balance** | Asymmetric: heavy figure on one side balanced by vast negative sky |
| 5 | **Proportion/Scale** | Figure small relative to the architecture — proportion is what makes cosmic scale legible |
| 6 | **Harmony** | The tight monochrome palette + single light source enforces visual unity |
| 7 | **Rhythm** | Progressive ripples + the candle's gold-dust suspended air |
| 8 | **Unity** | All Elements + Principles serve a single impression: *sorrow as guidance* (the Velvet Aeon thematic preserve) |
| 9 | **Variety** | The one warm specular highlight in an otherwise cool field — controlled disruption against the unified base |

### Drill ladder (one-week-to-permanent)

| Day | Drill | METER pass criterion |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Reconstruct the canonical scene from memory; name each of the 7 anchors aloud | All 7 Elements named in order in <30s |
| 3–4 | Reconstruct + name the 9 Principles by walking the scene and asking "why this design choice?" | All 9 Principles named in <60s |
| 5–6 | Apply the lens *outward*: pick any photograph or painting and name the 2–4 load-bearing Elements + the governing Principle | <60s per image, 5 images in a row |
| 7 | Apply the lens to a mental scene you have *not* pre-built: an arbitrary scene from a memory palace, identify which Elements and Principles it actually uses | Diagnose missing/weak Principles → propose a fix |

### METER pass criterion

Stand in front of any image (your own, public artwork, a photograph). Can you name all 7 Elements present (and which 2–3 are load-bearing) + identify the governing Principle in under 60 seconds? If yes, the canonical scene has stabilized as a portable reading lens — Scene Grammar is now reflexive.

## Stage 3 Domain Mapping

Every Element corresponds to a Stage 3 manipulation domain in [visualization-training](./visualization-training.md):

| Element | Stage 3 Domain | Key moves |
|---|---|---|
| **Line** | State / Structure | Contour trace → silhouette → implied edge → dissolve |
| **Shape** | State / Structure | 2D silhouette manipulation; geometric → organic morph |
| **Form** | Scale + State/Structure | Shape → Form inflation (add light source, shadow, depth); 3D deflation back to flat |
| **Value** | Color / Light | Value sweep: black → gray → white; value contrast control |
| **Color** | Color / Light | Full treatment: see [color-theory-mental-model](./color-theory-mental-model.md) |
| **Space** | Space / Position | Positive/negative space toggle; depth cue manipulation (overlap, scale, atmosphere) |
| **Texture** | Material / Phase *(texture sub-axis)* | Roughness sweep; reflectivity sweep; material grain; micro-zoom into texture |

## Principles as the Stage 3→4 Bridge

The Principles operate at the **multi-element composition level** — they are the rules for assembling several manipulated objects into a coherent scene. This makes them the natural bridge between:
- **Stage 3** (single-object manipulation) → where Elements drills live
- **Stage 4** (hub building) → where stable multi-element scenes are constructed and maintained

| Principle | Composition drill before Stage 4 |
|---|---|
| **Contrast** | Place two objects: one max-bright, one max-dark. The dark one recedes; the bright one advances. |
| **Emphasis** | From 5 neutral objects, spotlight one by making it the only vivid, bright, moving element. |
| **Balance** | 2-object asymmetric balance: one heavy-dark-large vs two light-small. Find the weight-equality point. |
| **Harmony** | Apply one color temperature to all objects in the scene. Watch them cohere. |
| **Rhythm** | 5 identical objects in progressive size decrease along a curved path. The path leads to the focal point. |
| **Unity** | A full 5-object scene where all Elements work together: one value range, one temperature, one emphasis point. |

Once these composition drills are fluent, Stage 4 hub building is not just "a room with objects" but a fully designed scene with deliberate emphasis, balance, and rhythm — which is why it holds up under the 1-week stability test.

## REMAPS Integration

| [REMAPS](./remaps.md) move | Scene Grammar application |
|---|---|
| **E — Exaggerate** | Push any Element to its extreme: maximum contrast, maximum texture roughness, maximum scale difference; amplify one Principle to override all others |
| **M — Modify** | Transform one Element into another: inflate Shape into Form (add light), degrade Form back to Shape (flatten), shift Value, swap Material for Texture |
| **A — Aesthetic** | Apply a Principle for beauty: balance the composition, add rhythmic movement, enforce harmony via shared palette |
| **S — Sensations** | Add the Tactile channel to Texture drills (feel the surface as you manipulate it); add the kinesthetic channel to Rhythm (feel the beat of the repeating element) |
| **P — Play** | Violate a Principle for comic effect: radical imbalance (one enormous object, everything else tiny), complete anti-harmony (every object a different clashing hue), reversed emphasis (the least important thing is the most vivid) |

## 3-Framework Image Generation Workflow

When generating a production image in ChatGPT (or any image model), run these three stages in order. Each stage is a distinct concern; all three are required for a first-or-second-try result.

| Stage | Framework | Question answered | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | [Scene Grammar](./scene-grammar.md) | *What is in the scene?* | 2–4 Elements selected + one Principle applied |
| **2** | [REMAPS](./remaps.md) | *Will the scene stick as a mnemonic encoding?* | Scene transformed by 2–3 moves (vivid, distinctive, retrievable) |
| **3** | [CLAMP](./clamp-render-lens.md) | *How should it be captured and rendered?* | Camera · Lighting · Aspect · Medium · Preserve/Proscribe filled |

### Stage 1 checklist (VAF — what's in the scene?)

Before touching REMAPS or CLAMP, answer two questions:

1. **Which Elements are load-bearing?** Pick 2–4 deliberately: Line · Shape · Form · Value · Color · Space · Texture. A scene with all seven equally weighted has no visual hierarchy — pick which ones carry the meaning.
2. **Which Principle governs the composition?** One governing rule is enough: **Contrast** for drama · **Emphasis** to fix a focal point · **Harmony** for unity · **Rhythm** for movement · **Balance** for visual weight.

A scene with deliberate Elements + one governing Principle gives REMAPS clear material to work with and CLAMP a coherent subject to capture.

### Stage 2 checklist (REMAPS — does it stick?)

Walk the six moves and apply at least 2–3:

- **R** Rotate / Reverse — odd viewpoint or inverted relation? (Feeds CLAMP C: a reversed perspective becomes a Dutch angle or extreme low-angle shot)
- **E** Exaggerate — push one Element to its extreme: maximum Value contrast, giant scale, extreme Texture roughness
- **M** Modify / Merge / Move — change a material or merge two images; add active motion (CLAMP M-slot names the era/style; REMAPS M picks *what transforms*)
- **A** Associate / Aesthetic — anchor to a known image; the Stage 1 Principle becomes the Aesthetic rule (Harmony → constrained palette; Contrast → one vivid element against muted field)
- **P** Place / Path — dock at a palace locus if this is also a mnemonic asset
- **S** Sensations — add touch/sound/smell hooks (these feed CLAMP L: a cold-damp sensation maps to overcast diffuse lighting; heat maps to golden-hour backlight)

### Stage 3 checklist (CLAMP — how is it captured?)

With the REMAPS scene in hand, fill the five slots:

- **C** Camera — angle + lens (the REMAPS Rotate move named the orientation; CLAMP names it: upside-down → Dutch angle, wide → establishing wide)
- **L** Lighting — named recipe (REMAPS Sensations named the mood; CLAMP names the setup: heat → golden-hour backlight, damp → overcast diffuse, candlelit room → low-key chiaroscuro)
- **A** Aspect + use-mode — output size and polish register (`1024×1536` portrait vs `1536×1024` landscape; concept art vs ad vs UI mock)
- **M** Medium + era anchor — rendering style; see [world-velvet-aeon](./world-velvet-aeon.md) for the default world profile
- **P** Preserve / Proscribe — invariants ("two figures must be legible") + negatives ("no watermark, no modern dress") + verbatim text if needed

### Prompt assembly order

```
[REMAPS scene sentence] , [CLAMP M: medium + era] , [CLAMP L: lighting] , [CLAMP C: camera/lens] , [CLAMP A: aspect/use-mode] .

Preserve: [invariants]
No: [negatives]
Text (verbatim): "[exact string]" — [typography directives]
```

Order matters: Medium + era anchor locks the model's visual register early; constraints go last so the model reads them as hard limits, not suggestions.

### VAF Elements → CLAMP slot mapping

| Element | CLAMP slot it primarily feeds |
|---|---|
| **Color / Value** | L (lighting recipe controls perceived color temperature and value range) |
| **Space** | C (camera distance and depth of field control positive/negative space) · A (aspect ratio controls spatial composition) |
| **Form / Shape** | C (camera angle determines whether 3D form reads as volume or silhouette) |
| **Texture / Material** | M (medium and era anchor carry the textural register: oil paint vs 3D render vs photography) |
| **Line** | C (lens focal length controls line convergence and distortion) |
