# Linguistic Crux

**Summary**: A **sub-class of [crux-move](./crux-move.md)** in which the crux operation is *re-examining a lexical assumption* — recognizing that the puzzle's pivotal word has a *specific* meaning the trap-reader has substituted with a *general* one (or vice-versa). Distinct from structural cruxes (where the operation is computation or insight) — the resolution here is **dictionary-level**, not arithmetic-level. Surfaced from the [livingstone-thomson-brain-teasers](./livingstone-thomson-brain-teasers.md) ingest 2026-05-24 — archetype O (~5 puzzles) repeatedly required re-reading a single word. Sister to [lateral-thinking-puzzles](./lateral-thinking-puzzles.md) trap class 3.

**Sources**:
- [livingstone-thomson-brain-teasers](./livingstone-thomson-brain-teasers.md) #100 Stringing Along, #417 Feet, #393 Egg Yolk, #332 Divisions
- [crux-move](./crux-move.md) — the parent concept
- Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations* (1953) §43 — *"the meaning of a word is its use in the language"* (the philosophical anchor for why these puzzles work)

**Last updated**: 2026-05-24

## The shape of a linguistic crux

A linguistic crux fires when:

1. The puzzle's surface text contains a **word with multiple senses** (or registers, or grammatical functions).
2. The puzzle is **set up to prime one sense** via narrative context.
3. The **correct answer requires the other sense**.

Unlike a structural crux, no computation will reveal the linguistic crux — only **re-reading the pivotal word with conscious attention to alternative meanings** will.

## Worked examples

### #100 Stringing Along

> *Imagine a piece of string. Now cut it at any point. You end up with a piece of string with two ends. How is this possible?*

**Pivot**: "piece of string". The trap-reader defaults to *open string* (cutting → 2 pieces, 4 ends). The puzzle requires *closed loop* (cutting → 1 piece, 2 ends). 

**Lexical move**: "piece" doesn't specify topology. Default-open is a *convention*, not the meaning.

### #417 Feet

> *A lumberjack keeps his timber in a shed. He has 14 feet of timber. One day, he takes 7 feet of timber and leaves. How many feet does he leave with?*

**Pivot**: "feet". Trap-reader: 7 feet (the timber). Puzzle: **9 feet** = 7 of timber + the lumberjack's own 2.

**Lexical move**: "feet" as unit of length ↔ "feet" as body parts. The narrative deliberately establishes the unit-of-length sense, then the *question* shifts the sense.

### #393 Egg Yolk

> *Which of these is correct: "The yolk of the egg are white" or "The yolk of the egg is white"?*

**Pivot**: *both* sentences are wrong (yolks are yellow). The puzzle is *not* asking which is grammatically right — it's asking which statement is *true*. Neither is.

**Lexical move**: "correct" ↔ grammatically correct vs factually correct. The puzzle exploits the assumption that a multiple-choice question has a correct option.

### #332 Divisions

> *If you divide 50 by a half and add 5, what is the answer?*

**Pivot**: "divide by a half". Trap-reader parses as "halve 50, then add 5" → 30. Correct: 50 ÷ 0.5 = 100 → 105.

**Lexical move**: "divide by a half" is the formal *operation* (divide by 0.5); "divide a half" or "halve" is the colloquial sense. The phrasing is unambiguous formally but easy to mis-parse colloquially.

### #96 Sunrise

> *Where in the world could you see the sunrise twice in any 24-hour period?*

**Pivot**: "twice". Trap-reader hunts for impossible geometric trick. Correct: *anywhere* during seasonal day-shortening, sunrise can occur near midnight then again 24h later... actually the book's official answer is more about "anywhere" via the seasonal shift. The pivot is "twice in 24 hours" being assumed to require special geography.

**Lexical move**: "twice" assumed to mean "within any arbitrarily chosen 24-hour window" → trivial via straddling the date.

## The 4-step recognition + resolution procedure

1. **Detect the pivot.** A linguistic crux is signaled by *exactly one word doing heavy semantic lifting*. If removing or substituting that word would change the answer entirely, it's the pivot.
2. **List the senses.** What are the 2-3 plausible meanings? Include grammatical function (noun vs verb), domain (unit vs body part), and register (formal vs colloquial).
3. **Check which sense the puzzle's setup primed.** Usually the puzzle establishes one sense via context.
4. **Try the answer assuming the *other* sense.** If it produces a clean answer, the linguistic crux fired.

## Why this works (Wittgensteinian frame)

Wittgenstein's *meaning is use* observation: words don't have *intrinsic* meanings, they have **use-patterns** in language-games. Most puzzles operate inside one language-game (math, physics, business, history). Linguistic-crux puzzles **switch language-games mid-sentence** without telling the reader. The crux is detecting the unannounced switch.

This isn't unique to brain teasers — it's the operating principle of puns, riddles, legal-loophole arguments, and most political euphemism. The wiki should treat this as a **transferable language skill**, not a puzzle-domain trick.

## Cross-link to [ants-and-lies-of-learning](./ants-and-lies-of-learning.md)

Linguistic cruxes are sustained by an **automatic positive assumption** — the assumption that the word means what context primed. Per the Amen identify-name-rebut protocol:

| Step | In puzzle-solving |
|---|---|
| Identify | Notice the pivot word; feel the reach for the primed meaning |
| Name | Burns/Beck distortion: *jumping to conclusions* (assuming the primed sense is correct) |
| Rebut | "What if this word means something else here?" Try the alternative reading |

The 3-step protocol is the operational defense against linguistic-crux ANTs.

## Cross-link to [crux-move](./crux-move.md) §"Where the crux can live"

Linguistic crux lives at the **Tool level** of [problem-solving-three-levels](./problem-solving-three-levels.md) — it's not a strategic plan or a tactical choice; it's the substitution of one specific dictionary lookup for another. Two operations:

- **Tactic-level move**: recognize this is a linguistic-crux puzzle (instead of a structural one)
- **Tool-level move**: perform the dictionary-substitution itself

The tactic-level move is the harder skill; once recognized, the substitution is trivial.

## When this is NOT the crux

- **Pure math puzzle with no narrative pivot.** Sequences, arithmetic, geometry — no lexical ambiguity.
- **Lateral red-herring puzzle** ([lateral-thinking-puzzles](./lateral-thinking-puzzles.md) trap class 2) — here the pivot isn't a word, it's *which tactic to use*. Adjacent but distinct trap.
- **Cultural-sequence puzzle** (archetype D) — pivot isn't lexical; it's stored-sequence lookup.

## Mnemonic

Velvet Aeon Mode-Identity register: a **scholar at a desk with a single open dictionary** under warm candlelight. Each page of the dictionary has *two columns* — one column shows the **primed meaning** (the sense the puzzle wants you to assume); the other shows the **alternative meaning** (the sense the puzzle actually requires). A **bookmark** marks the pivot word for each puzzle. The scholar's job is to **read the right column** under time pressure. When she reads the wrong column, the dictionary closes shut for that puzzle — irrecoverable. The scholar has the **FRAGILE** face archetype this time (luminous porcelain, freckles) — linguistic crux is a *fragile* recognition, not a power move; the alternative meaning is delicate and easily missed. Long hair flows over the dictionary; preserve = **sorrow as guidance** (each missed pivot is a tiny loss the scholar uses to sharpen next attempt). Single warm candle.

## U — See (CAST)

1. Scholar at desk with two-column dictionary under single candle, bookmark marking pivot
2. Edges: pivot word → two-column lookup → correct column → answer

## D — Name (NEDF)

1. Linguistic crux = re-read the pivot word with alternative meaning
2. Sub-class of [crux-move](./crux-move.md); lives at Tool level
3. Distinguisher: dictionary-level operation, not arithmetic
4. Failure mode: read the primed column without checking the alternative

## F — Do (SPEAR)

1. Detect pivot word (which word is doing heavy lifting?)
2. List 2-3 senses
3. Check which sense the setup primed
4. Try answer with the *other* sense; if clean, linguistic crux fired

## B — Watch (HEART)

1. Reading the puzzle once and committing to first interpretation
2. Confusing with lateral red-herring (different trap class)
3. Over-applying (assuming every puzzle has a pivot word — most don't)
4. Skipping the "list senses" step

## L — Predict (ORACLE)

1. Puzzles where a single word can be re-read non-trivially → likely linguistic
2. ~5 of 211 in the brain-teaser corpus; lower density in math-contest sources

## R — Act (GRACE)

1. Spot pivot → say "this might be a linguistic crux"
2. List the senses out loud
3. Coach another → ask "what does that word mean *here*?"
