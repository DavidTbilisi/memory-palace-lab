# IQ: History and Critique

**Summary**: IQ (Intelligence Quotient) is a score derived from standardized psychometric tests, first formalized by Alfred Binet (1905) for identifying children needing educational support, then weaponized through the 20th century as a proxy for fixed biological worth. The critique is not that IQ tests are useless — they predict school grades, job performance, and health outcomes better than most single measures — but that (1) they measure a *narrow slice* of cognitively relevant capacities, (2) the score is far more malleable than the "fixed biological trait" narrative admits (Flynn Effect, substrate effects), and (3) the history of the concept is entangled with eugenics in ways that introduced systematic measurement error and interpretive bias that persist. This page is the canonical owner; see [social-weathering](./social-weathering.md) and [epigenetics-and-intelligence](./epigenetics-and-intelligence.md) for upstream substrate critique.

## Brief history

| Year | Event | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| 1905 | Binet-Simon scale | Designed to *identify*, not rank; Binet explicitly warned against reifying the score as fixed intelligence |
| 1904 | Spearman's *g* | Factor analysis yields a general factor shared across test batteries; interpreted (controversially) as a unitary biological capacity |
| 1916 | Stanford-Binet (Terman) | Adds hereditarian framing; used to argue for selective immigration restriction in US Congress testimony |
| 1917 | Army Alpha/Beta tests | First mass IQ testing (1.7M WW1 recruits); data misread to argue for racial/national hierarchies |
| 1927 | *The Measurement of Intelligence* (Terman + Yerkes) | Peak eugenics-IQ entanglement; forced sterilization laws citing IQ data |
| 1969 | Jensen controversy | Arthur Jensen's *Harvard Educational Review* article arguing Black-white gap is ~80% heritable, sparking decades of debate |
| 1984 | Flynn Effect (Flynn) | IQ scores rising ~3 pts/decade globally over the 20th century — incompatible with "fixed biological trait" claim |
| 1994 | *The Bell Curve* (Herrnstein & Murray) | Revived hereditarian claims; immediately contested by APA task force 1996 |
| 1996 | APA task force report | Consensus: g is real and predictive, but group differences are not explained by genetics; heritability within groups ≠ between groups |
| 2012 | Nisbett et al. consensus | Adoption, nutrition, schooling, lead exposure all shift IQ substantially; environment is major driver |

## What IQ tests measure well

- **g (general factor)**: variance shared across all cognitive test batteries — spatial, verbal, numerical, memory — captures something real
- **Academic performance prediction**: r ≈ 0.50–0.65 with school grades
- **Job performance prediction**: r ≈ 0.30–0.50 for most occupations; higher for complex jobs
- **Health and longevity prediction**: higher IQ → longer life, even controlling for SES

These are not trivial. IQ is one of the most robust predictors in psychology. The critique is not "IQ tests are useless" but "they are partial, malleable, and have been misread."

## What IQ tests measure poorly

| Gap | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Emotional and social intelligence** | Goleman's EI, or more carefully, John Mayer's MSCEIT — these predict relationship quality and leadership, partially independently of *g* |
| **Practical / tacit intelligence** | Sternberg's work: "street smarts" predict occupational success over and above IQ in many domains |
| **Creative / divergent ability** | Threshold effect: above ~120, IQ no longer predicts creative output; other factors dominate |
| **Character, grit, self-regulation** | Heckman 2006: non-cognitive skills explain large variance in life outcomes *unconditional on IQ*; early-childhood interventions gain here |
| **Substrate posture** | A test taken with sleep deprivation, high cortisol, or high weathering baseline returns scores well below the "true" latent estimate — see [social-weathering](./social-weathering.md) |

## The Flynn Effect — the killer fact

IQ scores have risen **3 points per decade** across the 20th century in every country where longitudinal data exists. Over 80 years, that is a 24-point average gain — nearly 1.5 standard deviations.

Implications:
1. If IQ were a fixed biological trait, this is impossible. Mass biological evolution does not operate on 10-year timescales.
2. The gain is real (not test familiarity artifact) — it persists on Raven's Progressive Matrices (a supposedly "culture-free" test).
3. Likely drivers: better nutrition (especially iodine), reduced lead exposure, increased formal schooling, more abstract-problem-dense environments.
4. Implication for group comparisons: **the entire Black-white IQ gap of ~15 points is smaller than the gain a single American generation made from 1945 to 1990.** Environment is doing the heavy lifting.

James Flynn's own interpretation: IQ tests don't measure raw intelligence — they measure "the ability to use abstract problem-solving schemas," a skill that depends on practice and education. The 20th century made abstract problem-solving ubiquitous; scores rose.

## Heritability: the most misread concept

"IQ is 50–80% heritable" is reported as if it means "80% of your IQ is from genes." It does not.

**Heritability** is the proportion of *variance* in a *population* attributable to *genetic variation among individuals* in that population. Key constraints:
- It describes a population, not an individual
- It depends on environmental variance: in an environment with zero variance (everyone gets identical schooling, nutrition, etc.), heritability approaches 1.0 — but that tells you nothing about the role of environment in setting the mean
- **Heritability within a group says nothing about the cause of differences between groups** (Lewontin's 1970 flower-pot argument)

The Nisbett et al. 2012 review conclusion: "The heritability of IQ is substantially lower in impoverished environments than in affluent ones." Meaning: genetic potential is a ceiling; the environment determines how close to the ceiling the outcome lands.

## Measurement error amplified by substrate

[social-weathering](./social-weathering.md) adds a substrate layer: a test administered under high allostatic load, poor sleep, or high cortisol systematically under-measures the latent cognitive capacity. Populations bearing higher weathering baselines therefore produce systematically depressed scores — this is **measurement error**, not a biological finding. The gap gets reported as a cognitive-capacity difference when it is in part a substrate-posture difference.

Correcting for substrate posture (sleep, nutrition, stress load, cortisol regulation) before drawing conclusions from group IQ data is not speculative — the mechanism is documented through [bdnf-and-neurogenesis](./bdnf-and-neurogenesis.md) and the cortisol-hippocampus literature.

## Failure modes

| Failure | What it produces |
|---|---|
| **Reification** | Treating the score as measuring a unitary fixed thing called "intelligence" — Binet's own warning, ignored |
| **Heritability → fixed** | Misreading heritability as "cannot be changed by environment" |
| **Within-group → between-group** | Assuming within-group heritability explains between-group differences |
| **Score = capacity** | Ignoring substrate effects, measurement conditions, stereotype threat |
| **Above-threshold thinking** | Assuming IQ is the dominant predictor at all levels; past ~120 it explains much less variance in outcomes |
