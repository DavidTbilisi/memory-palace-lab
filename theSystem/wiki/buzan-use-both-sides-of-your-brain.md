# Use Both Sides of Your Brain (Buzan)

**Summary**: *Use Both Sides of Your Brain* (Tony Buzan, E. P. Dutton 1974, multiple revisions, ~170 pp) is **the first popular publication of [mind mapping](./buzan-mind-map-mastery.md)** — the origin document for a technique the wiki still uses, wrapped in a theoretical framing the wiki does not accept. Buzan motivated the method through the **left-brain / right-brain dichotomy** popular in 1970s science writing: linear note-taking supposedly engaged only the "logical" hemisphere, while colour, image, and radial layout recruited the "creative" one. The technique survived fifty years of use; **the hemisphere story did not survive the neuroscience**. This page is the source-summary / routing owner, and it exists partly to keep that split visible — mind mapping itself is defined on [buzan-mind-map-mastery](./buzan-mind-map-mastery.md), Buzan's later and fuller consolidation.

## Why this book has a page at all

Because it is the **origin**, and because origins carry framings that later get quietly dropped. [buzan-mind-map-mastery](./buzan-mind-map-mastery.md) (2018) is the better reference for how to actually build a mind map — it is Buzan's final consolidation and it owns the 7 Laws. What the 1974 book supplies that the 2018 one does not is the **original argument**: why Buzan thought linear notes were the problem, and what he believed mind mapping was doing to the brain.

Keeping that argument on the record matters for a wiki that cites the technique. A reader who meets mind mapping through Buzan meets a mechanism story attached to it, and needs to know which half the wiki stands behind.

## What held up, and what did not

| | Buzan's 1974 claim | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Technique** | Central image, radial branches, one keyword per branch, colour and image throughout | **Held.** Used across the wiki; owned by [buzan-mind-map-mastery](./buzan-mind-map-mastery.md) |
| **Mechanism** | Linear notes engage the "left brain"; colour/image/radial layout recruits the underused "right brain" | **Did not hold.** Hemisphere specialisation is real but far narrower and more distributed than the popular dichotomy; the 1970s split-brain framing does not support the claim |

The honest reading is that Buzan was **right about the practice and wrong about why**. That is a common and unembarrassing pattern in method books, and the wiki's response is to keep the practice and route mechanism questions elsewhere: [willingham-cognitive-principles](./willingham-cognitive-principles.md) is the calibration point for claims about how learning works, and the reason the technique helps is better explained by ordinary cognitive-science mechanisms already owned here — [elaboration](./elaboration.md) (a branch forces you to name a relationship), [generation-effect](./generation-effect.md) (you produce the structure rather than receive it), and [serial-position-curve](./serial-position-curve.md) (a distinctive visual item resists the middle sag).

## The wider pattern this page is an instance of

A method can be effective while its stated mechanism is wrong, because the method was arrived at by trial and the mechanism by storytelling. The wiki has the same shape on file elsewhere — most directly in [learning-styles-myth](./learning-styles-myth.md), where a widely-adopted framing (match instruction to a preferred modality) has no empirical support even though the *activities* it inspired are often fine on other grounds.

The discipline that follows: **evaluate the technique and the mechanism separately.** Dropping a working technique because its explanation is wrong is as much an error as keeping a wrong explanation because the technique works.

## Mnemonic

**The map survived; the brain story didn't.** Buzan's 1974 book gave the wiki a technique it still uses and an explanation it doesn't. Two columns, judged apart — that is the whole page.
