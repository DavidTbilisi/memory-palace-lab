# Analogical Reasoning (Copi Ch 11)

**Summary**: The inductive argument form in which the conclusion about a new case is supported by **observed similarities** to known cases. [Copi](./copi-introduction-to-logic.md) Ch 11 lists **six criteria** for analogical argument strength: number of instances · variety · relevance · disanalogies · claim modesty · number of similarities. The *first* of Copi's inductive-reasoning chapters (Ch 11-14); the bridge from deductive validity to inductive strength. **Inductive cousin of [BRIDGE LOAD](./bridge-load.md)**: BRIDGE LOAD constructs analogies for *comprehension*; this page evaluates analogies for *argument strength*.

## One-line

> *Two cases share features A, B, C; the first also has feature D; therefore the second probably has D.* Strength = function of six criteria. Never *valid* (deductively); strong arguments can be inductively *strong* or *weak* depending on the criteria.

## Unlocks (lead, not footer)

1. **Six-criteria checklist for evaluating any analogy as an *argument*.** When you encounter *"X is like Y, therefore expect Z about X"*, the six criteria let you grade the argument's strength quickly: more instances (1), more variety (2), more relevance (3), fewer disanalogies (4), more modest conclusion (5), more similarities (6) → stronger. Each criterion is a separable dial. **The argument is not a single yes/no; it's a six-dimensional grading.**

2. **Analogical arguments are *inductive*, not deductive.** Per [validity-vs-soundness](./validity-vs-soundness.md): they are evaluated for *strength*, not validity. No matter how many features two cases share, the conclusion about the next feature can be wrong. This is *not* a defect; it's the form's nature. The wiki cross-links to [ORACLE](./oracle-overview.md) distributional mode: analogical inference is a low-confidence prediction, not a guarantee.

3. **Analogies *also* serve [comprehension](./bridge-load.md)** — the wiki's [BRIDGE LOAD](./bridge-load.md) protocol uses analogies to *teach*, not to *argue*. The Copi criteria apply when an analogy is being used *as a premise to support a conclusion*; the BRIDGE protocol applies when an analogy is being used *to make a concept retrievable*. **Same construct, two purposes; same six criteria, different scoring.**

4. **Disanalogies as the load-bearing single criterion.** Copi notes that *disanalogies* (relevant differences between the cases) are often the most-overlooked factor. A salesman compares his product to a competitor on three favorable axes and skips four unfavorable ones; the argument can look strong while being weak. **The reflex: when you encounter an analogical argument, immediately list disanalogies.**

## Mnemonic

**N-V-R-D-M-S** = *Number · Variety · Relevance · Disanalogies · Modesty · Similarities.*

Six criteria. Read as *"Navy ride medics"* or *"N-V-R-D-M-S"* directly. Six dials to grade an analogy on.

## Memory checksum

1. **State the six criteria.** (1: Number of instances; 2: Variety of instances; 3: Relevance of similarities; 4: Disanalogies — fewer is stronger; 5: Modesty of conclusion — less ambitious is stronger; 6: Number of similarities.)
2. **What kind of argument is analogical?** (Inductive — evaluated for *strength*, never for validity. Conclusions are *probable*, not guaranteed.)
3. **Which criterion is most-overlooked?** (Disanalogies. The reflex: when you encounter an analogical argument, immediately list disanalogies.)
4. **Distinguish analogical-as-argument from analogical-as-comprehension.** (As argument: the analogy supports a conclusion (Copi Ch 11; six criteria for strength). As comprehension: the analogy makes a concept retrievable (BRIDGE LOAD; the analogy's purpose is teaching, not arguing).)
5. **Why is the "modesty of conclusion" criterion?** (A modest conclusion (small claim) is more strongly supported than an ambitious one from the same evidence. *"Probably also has D"* is stronger than *"definitely has D and E and F"* from the same set of similarities.)

## Visual — the six-criteria scorecard

**Analogical argument evaluation** — each criterion is a dial from a strong pole to a weak pole:

| # | Criterion | Strong pole | Weak pole |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Number of instances | many | few |
| 2 | Variety of instances | diverse | uniform |
| 3 | Relevance of features | relevant | irrelevant |
| 4 | Disanalogies | few | many |
| 5 | Modesty of conclusion | modest | ambitious |
| 6 | Number of similarities | many | few |

*Score each criterion independently (1–5). Sum + judgment → argument strength. **Not a validity test** — the argument can be strong yet false (inductive nature).*

The scorecard is the operational form of Copi Ch 11. **Run it on every analogical argument you encounter.**

## The six criteria in detail

### Criterion 1 — Number of instances

The more instances exhibit the pattern, the stronger the analogical inference. *"Three Toyotas I've owned have lasted 200,000 miles"* is stronger evidence about Toyota durability than *"one Toyota I've owned has lasted 200,000 miles"*.

### Criterion 2 — Variety of instances

The instances should differ in irrelevant ways. *"Three Toyotas (one Corolla 2010, one Camry 2015, one Highlander 2020) have lasted 200,000 miles"* is stronger than *"three Corollas from 2010 have lasted 200,000 miles"*. Variety controls for confounding factors that would be invisible in a uniform sample.

### Criterion 3 — Relevance of similarities

The similarities shared with the case-of-interest should be *relevant* to the property being predicted. If you predict that a new Toyota will be reliable based on the similar Toyotas you've owned, *"made by Toyota"* is more relevant than *"red colored"*. **Irrelevant similarities don't strengthen the argument**, no matter how many of them you list.

### Criterion 4 — Disanalogies (most-overlooked)

Relevant *differences* weaken the argument. If you're predicting that a new Toyota will last 200,000 miles, but the new Toyota has a hybrid drivetrain you've never owned, the disanalogy weakens the argument. Copi: the single most-overlooked criterion; many bad analogies pass two-of-three favorable criteria but fail on disanalogies that the arguer doesn't mention.

### Criterion 5 — Modesty of conclusion

The same evidence supports a modest conclusion more strongly than an ambitious one. *"Probably reliable"* from three Toyota cases is stronger than *"will last 250,000 miles with no major repairs"* from the same three cases. **Reducing your conclusion's claim strengthens the argument from any given evidence base.**

### Criterion 6 — Number of similarities

More relevant similarities → stronger analogy. *"Same make, same model, same engine, same care regime"* is stronger than just *"same make"*.

## Worked example (Copi-style)

The argument:

> *I've owned three Toyota Camrys (2010, 2014, 2018), all in varied climates (Florida humid, Arizona desert, Wisconsin winter), all driven 150,000+ miles, all reaching 200,000+ miles with only routine maintenance. I'm about to buy a 2026 Toyota Camry. Therefore my new Camry will probably last 200,000+ miles with only routine maintenance.*

Scoring:

| Criterion | Score (1-5) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| (1) Number of instances | 4 | Three is decent; more would help |
| (2) Variety of instances | 5 | Three different climates, three different model years; good coverage |
| (3) Relevance of similarities | 4 | Camry as a model, Toyota as a manufacturer, similar maintenance regime — relevant |
| (4) Disanalogies | 3 | Possible model changes 2018 → 2026; possible mechanical revisions; the 8-year gap is a meaningful disanalogy not mentioned |
| (5) Modesty of conclusion | 4 | *"Probably last 200K with routine maintenance"* is modest enough |
| (6) Number of similarities | 4 | Make · model · maintenance regime · driving conditions — four relevant similarities |

Sum: 24/30. **Reasonably strong argument**. Improvements possible: list potential disanalogies (e.g., 2024+ Toyota redesign), increase instance count.

## Strong analogies vs misleading analogies

| Strong | Misleading |
|---|---|
| Predicting drug efficacy from related compounds (relevant chemical similarity) | Predicting drug efficacy from related cultural origins (irrelevant similarity) |
| Predicting market behavior from prior cycles (variety of conditions; relevant economic similarities) | Predicting market behavior from sports metaphors (no relevance) |
| Predicting code reliability from similar algorithms (relevant structural similarity) | Predicting code reliability from team-size similarities (often irrelevant) |
| Predicting biological function from homologous structures (deep relevance via shared ancestry) | Predicting biological function from analogous structures (convergent but not homologous; weaker) |

The pattern: relevance and disanalogies do most of the work; counting similarities does less.

## Cross-link to the wiki

### [BRIDGE LOAD](./bridge-load.md)

The wiki's BRIDGE LOAD protocol uses analogies for *comprehension* — making an unfamiliar concept retrievable by mapping onto a familiar one. The six criteria from Copi apply to BRIDGE-style analogies as **a quality check**:

| Copi criterion | BRIDGE LOAD application |
|---|---|
| (1) Number of instances | Have you tested the analogy on multiple cases? |
| (2) Variety of instances | Does the analogy survive across varied applications? |
| (3) Relevance of similarities | Are the mapped features *structurally* similar (not just surface-similar)? |
| (4) Disanalogies | Does the BRIDGE template's *forbidden-transfer* slot identify them? |
| (5) Modesty of conclusion | Is the analogy being used to teach a *partial* mapping, not a complete identity? |
| (6) Number of similarities | Does the mapping table cover multiple slots (Name-hook · Essence · Distinguisher · Failure)? |

**A BRIDGE LOAD analogy that scores well on Copi's six criteria is *also* a good comprehension analogy.** The two purposes share the same quality dimensions.

### [ORACLE](./oracle-overview.md) distributional mode

Analogical reasoning is structurally [ORACLE](./oracle-overview.md)'s distributional mode: estimate a probability distribution over outcomes given partial information about a new case. The Copi six criteria are quality factors for the ORACLE distributional inference.

### [problem-solving-os](./problem-solving-os.md)

Step 3 of [PS-OS](./problem-solving-os.md) (Choose-Method) can fire on an *analogy* (this problem is like X → try X's method). The six criteria gate the analogical move: if criteria are weak, the method-transfer is unreliable and you should classify the problem freshly.

## Failure modes

- **Cherry-picked similarities** (criterion 6 gamed; criterion 4 ignored).
- **Surface similarities mistaken for structural ones** (criterion 3 failed).
- **Ambitious conclusion from modest evidence** (criterion 5 failed; the most common rhetorical move in popular essays).
- **Single instance treated as a pattern** (criterion 1 failed; the "anecdote as data" fallacy).
- **Uniform instances treated as varied** (criterion 2 failed; the "three students all from the same high school" sampling problem).
